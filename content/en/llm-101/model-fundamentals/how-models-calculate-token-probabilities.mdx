In the previous lesson, we looked at how a model [selects a token](/courses/llm-101/why-model-answers-vary) from a probability distribution. This time, I want to explain <JimmyNoteAnchor id="token-probabilities">how the model produces that distribution</JimmyNoteAnchor>.

<JimmyNote anchor="token-probabilities">
  This lesson involves mathematics that may seem dry or difficult. I hope the analogy helps you understand the principle. It may not explain every detail perfectly, but I prefer to give you the overall framework first. For a more precise and detailed explanation, consult other tutorials or relevant books.
</JimmyNote>

## 1. Understand input and parameters with one formula

Start with a very simple formula:

$$
y = ax + b
$$

x is the input, y is the output, and a and b are parameters. Their values determine how the formula processes the input.

## 2. Substitute the input to calculate candidate scores

Return to the third lesson’s example about what to bring when it is raining. I want to explain it from a different angle. Set aside complex probability theory and use basic multiplication and addition.

Suppose the output is “umbrella”, but the input varies: “It is sunny today. Remember your…” and “It is raining today. Remember your…”.

The difference is “sunny” versus “raining”. Simplify this to 0 for sun and 1 for rain. In other words, x(rain) = 1 and x(sun) = 0. Suppose training has also given us the parameters a = 2 and b = 1.

Substitute these into the formula to get the score for “umbrella”:

| Weather | Input | Calculation | Umbrella score |
| --- | --- | --- | ---: |
| Rain | `x = 1` | `2 × 1 + 1` | **3** |
| Sun | `x = 0` | `2 × 0 + 1` | **1** |

For now, think of the score as a rough analogy for probability. When the input says “raining”, “umbrella” is more likely to follow “Remember your”.

If “umbrella” has a score formula, y(umbrella) = a(umbrella)x + b(umbrella), other tokens, such as “raincoat” and “hat”, can have their own formulas. Only their a and b values differ.

| Candidate | a | b | Substitution | Score |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | ---: |
| umbrella | 2 | 1 | `2 × 1 + 1` | **3** |
| raincoat | 1 | 1 | `1 × 1 + 1` | **2** |
| hat | -2 | 3 | `-2 × 1 + 3` | **1** |

## 3. Convert scores to probabilities

One input and several calculations give us three numbers. A mathematical conversion called [softmax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Softmax_function) turns them into probabilities that add up to 100%. You do not need the mathematical details here. Candidates with higher scores receive higher probabilities.

| Candidate | Calculated score | Converted probability |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| umbrella | 3 | about **66.5%** |
| raincoat | 2 | about **24.5%** |
| hat | 1 | about **9.0%** |

## 5. Why is this useful?

**First, input affects the probabilities the model calculates.**

With the same parameters, changing “sunny” to “raining” changes the score for “umbrella”. In practice, the context, requirements, and examples you provide also enter the model’s calculations and affect subsequent token probabilities.

As I explained in [How does a large language model generate an answer?](/courses/llm-101/what-a-language-model-does), good results require enough information and correct information. Even with today’s models, clearly stating your goal, context, and requirements has a noticeable effect.

**Second, a token’s probability is not the probability that an answer is correct.**

The 66.5% probability for “umbrella” describes its chance of being chosen as the next token in this simplified example. It does not mean “the advice to bring an umbrella has a 66.5% chance of being correct.” Nor does it measure the model’s confidence in the whole answer.

A passage can be fluent and sound certain while still containing errors. Check sources when you need to confirm facts. Asking the model to sound more confident does not make its facts more reliable.
